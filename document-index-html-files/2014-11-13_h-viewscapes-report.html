Background The Town of Ladysmith is considering an expansion of its boundaries into lands to the northwest of town. This land is presently owned by Timberwest. An area of 287 hectares has been demarcated for this purpose. Figure 1 shows the present town boundaries and the expansion lands. The goal of this report is to analyse the potential visibility of development on the expansion lands. To accomplish that a series of locations within the expansion lands and mid-harbour were identified, as well as one from the other side of the inlet. At each location a series of images was acquired to create a panorama of the viewscapes. The latitude and longitude was recorded along with the compass bearing of the panoramic images. Using the existing Lidar data Timberwest then prepared slope profiles of the initial locations, as well as several others, in order to show which parts of the slopes were or were not readily visible from afar. Page 17 Looking at the images and slope profile data it is clear that very little of the Expansion Lands are clearly visible from within the boundaries of the Town. Even outside the Town boundaries the views are very limited when looking upwards towards the Expansion lands. From within the Expansion land areas the lower areas adjacent to the existing town boundaries would have an obvious view of the houses already developed, and vice versa. However, the topography in many of these lower areas is relatively flat, so there would not be highly visible development from many locations. It is clear from the slope profile analysis that visibility into the Expansion Land area is generally blocked by topography that is well beyond the Expansion lands. In effect, adjacent land forms block most views of what is or might occur within the Expansion Land area. Managing the Trees Within the Expansion Lands Given that the views into the Expansion Lands are blocked or limited by land beyond its borders, there is a question as to how views out of the land area can be managed. Much of the land base within the Expansion Lands have been logged within the past few decades. Regenerating forest covers most of the eastern portion, and even the higher elevation lands are now regrowing new trees. There are also patches of standing timber, most of which appears to be second growth and less than one hundred years old. There are several aspects to consider in deciding how to best manage the remaining standing timber. Firstly, it is assumed that riparian areas would be managed to maintain their biological function. That would require approved setbacks along the banks. Typically, in a forest management setting, these can be variable in width, and because there is no target of concern, it would not matter if edge trees fall down over time. In the Expansion land setting, much of the land base would eventually be developed for residential, institutional, or commercial uses, thus creating the possibility that there would be targets, such as property or people, within striking range of the edge trees. While exact prescriptions are not possible at the larger landscape level, some general principles should be considered. 1 Use the riparian areas as a network of green linkages across the landscape. They should form the framework of the green infrastructure, and can be a starting point for trail networks linking roads and development areas. 2 Consider the topography and whether it is feasible to create trails within narrow riparian corridors. If necessary, widen the riparian setback to accommodate trails, and retain more trees to maintain the mature forest ‘feel’ of these areas. Secondly, it is clearly desirable that potential development areas have views available. Some of these would be limited and might only extend to a local or street level, especially on the areas of flat ground. Other areas have tremendous potential for more distant views, and these should be carefully considered, not only to maximise the view potential, but also to consider how some of the land base might be retained for trails, parks, and natural areas. There are some areas, such as those by Viewpoint 5, where the standing timber could be removed along the ridge line to create excellent views to the east and across to distant views of the inlet. One key consideration will the time factor. If the upper parts of the land base are not likely to be developed for say ten to fifteen years, perhaps even longer, then the affects of logging now, would be considerably reduced by the time development takes place. The new forest that would regenerate could then be managed as green space, with some permanently cleared for play parks, while other areas could be groomed and managed

When planning these factors care will be needed to place the potential forest areas in locations where they are a) large enough to have some ecological and aesthetic function while b) not creating long term view blockages. In principle the sloping areas provide opportunities to create a combination of excellent long term views, while at the same time allowing for pockets of land to be retained for growing trees and creating a reasonable network of ecologically functional green infrastructure. Most of that design work requires more site specific landform analysis and planning before more detailed outcomes can be seen. Overall, it is not anticipated that removal of the standing timber will radically alter the visibility of the Expansion Land areas from anywhere within or immediately beyond the Town of Ladysmith. The only areas directly affected are the existing development along the north west edge of town, and because the land there is relatively flat in most of the Expansion Lands, the visual impact of new development is limited to a local level. Conclusions Analysis of the Expansion Land area shows that there are very few parts of the land base that are or will ever be visible from within the boundaries of the Town of Ladysmith. Even beyond the boundaries the lands are seldom readily visible because other areas of land in front of (i.e. closer to the viewer) the Expansion Lands block the view. Views from within the Expansion Lands are variable and some will be local and short, others will be more distant. Some of these views will be altered by the regenerating forest trees which may grow up and block distant views. Other views are currently blocked by standing timber, but if that is harvested, new views could be opened up to create very desirable viewscapes for residential uses. The exact details of potential views would need to be analysed in more detail before site specific prescriptions can be developed, and that work needs to be linked to the overall design of green space and other factors such as trail networks. Regardless of that more detailed work, additional timber harvesting is unlikely to radically alter the potential views of the Expansion Land area from viewpoints in or adjacent to the Town of Ladysmith. Recommendations As this proposal moves forward it would be useful to develop a land use plan that considers the following ideas. 1 There should be a network of parks, green spaces, trails and publicly accessible viewpoints t hroughout the landscape. 2 The riparian area corridors should be used as the core green infrastructure set aside from o ther development. Additional open space areas should be added as a complement to the core lands. 3 A ny timber harvesting contemplated should be designed to leave the core green infrastructure intact, and should be u ndertaken relatively soon, so that there is some certainty about the landscape being considered for expansion. 4 O nce the harvesting is completed, there may be a need to remove selected trees in the ensuing years, if, as d evelopment proceeds, some trees, such as edge trees or trees that have subsequently died or become unstable, p ose an unacceptable level of risk to planned or actual land uses within striking range.
