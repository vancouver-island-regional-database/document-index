Typical house design Early m iner’s h om es Ladysmith Heritage Homes and Neighbourhoods Ladysmith was an “instant town” , founded by coal baron James Dunsmuir. Oyster Harbour, as the area was previously called, became the shipping port for Dunsmuir’s coal mine at Extension, about 12 km to the north. The townsite was planned in 1899 as a tidy grid pattern facing the bay. Streets were named after British Officers of the Boer War, victorious in recapturing the town of Ladysmith, South Africa, in the year 1900. Recycling for over a Century Miner’s homes and harbour Miners were required to uproot and relocate in the newly built town of Ladysmith. As was typical of the time, entire buildings were moved to new locations. Many homes, shops, hotels and even churches were dismantled and shipped by railway from the nearby mining towns of Wellington and Extension. The dismantled buildings arrived in Ladysmith and were hauled up White Street, where the slope was less severe, using a narrow gauge rail line. A ‘steam donkey’ was used for a winch. From the hillside, the miner’s houses were transported by horse and wagon to their new lots, and reassembled on temporary wooden foundations. This area of town is still known to some as Donkey Hill. Most early neighbourhoods were thus made up of “recycled” modest miner’s cottages with the characteristic sloped roof at the back. From 1906 to the 1920’s the basic house shape changed to the hip-style roof, still visible throughout town. Following the closure of the Granby mine in the Cassidy area in the late 1930’s, some homes with fancier architectural details were also “recycled” and moved to Ladysmith.
